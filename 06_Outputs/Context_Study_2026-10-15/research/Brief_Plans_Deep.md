## Carbon and adaptation at district scale

**Source finding:** The brief links mitigation and adaptation across buildings, open spaces, public spaces and infrastructure. Its platform covers building and vehicle energy and carbon. Kajicho 1 & 2 is the detailed primary area; Kanda–Nihonbashi is an approximate wider area, with a 2050 horizon.

**Local interpretation:** The local questions concern both energy used by daily activities and continuity of walking, staying and services during heat or flooding. These can share a building or street while needing different evidence.

**Design response:** Start with a scope map and a system-boundary table for building electricity and fuels, vehicle activity and street exposure. Link each analysis to a design decision. The team defines the transport boundary; this stage adds no CFD.

**Scope:** Course scope; the outer boundary is approximate.

**Still to check:** Confirm the outer boundary, transport scope, scenarios and metrics.

## Prioritize building energy while protecting daily users

**Source finding:** The 2021 ward mitigation plan targets a 42.3% cut in energy-related CO₂ by 2030 from 2013; business uses accounted for about 79% in its 2018 baseline. The adaptation plan prioritizes vulnerable users, cooled rest spaces, warnings and shade, while asking that wider environmental effects be checked.

**Local interpretation:** This supports a building focus in office-commercial Kajicho, but the 79% is a dated ward figure, requiring local bills and use checks. Street adaptation asks who is exposed, when, and whether usable relief is available; canopy area or temperature alone cannot answer that.

**Design response:** Pair the energy baseline and assumptions with accessible, open and maintained heat-relief spaces. Record cooling electricity and check effects on walking space and neighboring drainage. Use the ward’s 42.3% as context, not as an automatic reduction requirement for every building.

**Scope:** The 2021 ward plans; local application needs Kajicho energy and user evidence.

**Still to check:** Check actual energy, heat-vulnerable users’ needs and current access to cool spaces.

## A model that connects decisions to action

**Source finding:** Planning Support uses a platform to develop issues, visions and actions. Implementation Support organizes businesses, government, academics and citizens to share a plan, act and propose public support. The required outputs are an explanatory booklet and a summary PowerPoint.

**Local interpretation:** A result needs an actor, resources and maintenance; an activity proposal needs a local problem and evidence. Every proposal therefore needs both a spatial explanation and a delivery explanation beside its drawings. The brief names Watage and the business-owner/academic CWL initiative; use those existing relationships to verify needs and roles.

**Design response:** Use one spread per action: issue, baseline map, vision, spatial measure, comparison, actors, sequence and open decisions. The site supports shared evidence, the booklet keeps the argument, and the slides communicate the key judgments.

**Scope:** Explicit course requirements; no quantitative grading rubric is supplied.

**Still to check:** The team must decide roles, pilots, costs and metric weights.

## The core area intersects two planning strategies

**Source finding:** Printed page 184 includes part of Kajicho 2 in precinct ③, emphasizing access to three stations, third places and a balance of housing and business. Precinct ④ includes parts of Kajicho 1 and 2 and focuses on the east Kanda Station–Nihonbashi mixed district, connections, green space and resilient hubs.

**Local interpretation:** The core is not a single uniform renewal area. Its northern links and east-station–Nihonbashi links have different emphases. The word “part” prevents a simple one-chome/one-precinct split or substitution of wider-area statistics for core evidence.

**Design response:** Overlay the course core, the reference precinct boundaries and streets. State which direction each segment addresses: links and places to pause between stations, or active edges, green nodes and public passages toward Nihonbashi. Check parcel controls separately.

**Scope:** Precinct ③: part of Kajicho 2; precinct ④: parts of Kajicho 1 and 2.

**Still to check:** A formal GIS overlay is pending; the policy diagram is explanatory, not a surveyed boundary.

## Plan change across different time horizons

**Source finding:** The 2021 Tokyo area master plan uses the 2040s for the urban vision and 2030 for major facilities. The final 2025 Tokyo strategy values people, history and local character. The 2026 interim Grand Design identifies unused potential in small buildings, loss of distinctive streets and uniform station areas.

**Local interpretation:** A 2050 horizon can include long-term change without turning every small block into a single redevelopment project. Small buildings and multiple actors invite comparison of trials, routine retrofits and measures needing sustained coordination. This is a studio inference.

**Design response:** Show baseline, reversible trials, building maintenance or renewal, and longer-term area coordination side by side. Give each a trigger and actor. Keep official horizons distinct from studio dates, interim documents and neighboring project schedules.

**Scope:** Tokyo-wide directions; the course supplies the local 2050 horizon.

**Still to check:** Phases are studio proposals; the 2026 document is interim, and the 2021 live PDF returned 403.

## Link building retrofit with a network of cooler places

**Source finding:** The ward plan notes a gap between individual developments and area-wide carbon action. For Akihabara–Kanda–Jimbocho, page 107 emphasizes connected cool spots, open space, building arrangement and retrofit. Existing district-heating assets are discussed for other specific areas, not established for Kajicho.

**Local interpretation:** For Kajicho, one renewal opportunity can connect equipment, envelope or roof work, ground-floor access and nearby rest space. Area cooperation depends on willing actors and energy evidence; a network diagram alone does not establish infrastructure.

**Design response:** Build a building–street opportunity table: use, energy evidence, maintenance timing, options, affected spaces and actors. Identify opportunities now, estimate savings when bills and operating conditions are available, and record shade by place and time.

**Scope:** Area-specific direction for Akihabara–Kanda–Jimbocho; local measures are derived proposals.

**Still to check:** Actual energy, equipment, networks and participation remain unknown; policy potential is not installed capacity.

## Make limited green space useful at street level

**Source finding:** The green plan reports 2018 coverage of 3.58% in Izumibashi versus 23.22% for Chiyoda. It links limited planting space to small plots and narrow alleys. Its measures include frontage, roof and wall greening, social use, rainwater functions and shared maintenance.

**Local interpretation:** These figures motivate dispersed small-space opportunities but do not measure Kajicho’s 2026 canopy. Roof area, visible greenery and accessible shade are different quantities; more green area does not automatically create a useful lunchtime rest place.

**Design response:** Screen frontages, corners, roofs and temporary plots using sun, access hours, clear width, drainage and maintenance. Show canopy, usable places to pause and roof opportunities separately, and retain missing tree locations as field tasks.

**Scope:** Historic Izumibashi figures and ward greening policy; inspect individual candidate sites.

**Still to check:** Check canopy, soil and utilities, roof capacity, access and maintenance.

## Translate local character into street-level decisions

**Source finding:** The brief locates Kajicho’s identity in hardware trade, crafts and later office-commercial activity. The Kanda guide translates history into brick viaducts, shrines, alleys, façades and continuous height, frontage and wall alignment. Its July 2020 list identifies the Yamanashi Chuo Bank branch and Taiyo/Maruishi Building in Kajicho.

**Local interpretation:** Character becomes observable through entrances, cornices, setbacks, transitions into alleys and how new equipment or planting meets old façades. Assess a street sequence, rather than only an isolated landmark.

**Design response:** Draw continuous street elevations and sections, separating retained proportions from adaptable elements. Compare energy retrofit with ground-floor visibility and alley connections. Keep the inventory date explicit and survey current use and condition.

**Scope:** Kanda guidance; two named resources lie in Kajicho 1.

**Still to check:** Verify present use, condition and ownership; drawings do not supply conservation or construction permission.

## Use Nihonbashi for connections and comparison

**Source finding:** The 2021 river vision connects ground, underground, bridgehead and waterside routes with building renewal, open space, energy cooperation and district continuity. The 2022 Nanobe vision addresses Kabutocho–Kayabacho: office-heavy uses, limited places to stay and difficult renewal of small blocks.

**Local interpretation:** For Kajicho, ask whether routes to Nihonbashi are continuous, where people can stay and who uses them at different times. Compare how small-owner renewal adds up to district improvement. Finance-hub programs and riverfront conditions retain their own geographic scope.

**Design response:** Show wider links toward bridges and the river, then resolve them into core crossings, street segments and ground-floor uses. Separate transferable mechanisms, local conditions to test and neighbor-specific programs. Treat archived project schedules as historical context.

**Scope:** Official Chuo vision areas, used as wider links and comparison.

**Still to check:** Current construction progress, cross-ward barriers and development conditions were not refreshed in this study.
