Kajicho workshop report

Urban Design Studio 2026 · Theme B · 24 November 2026

The studio discussed turning its six interim studies into a block renewal plan for 2050. The design area is Kajicho 1 and 2 within the solid Primary Boundary in Figure 5 of the Theme B brief. The wider Kanda–Nihonbashi area is used to check street and drainage connections.

Summary

  1. Develop one proposal from the interim studies. The instructors suggested keeping the evidence and the issues already identified, then organizing proposals around the places and systems the team can change. Draw buildings, public space and transport together on one map before assigning parts of the final presentation.
  2. Work with five blocks. Keep feasible renewal choices for each block and select a few combinations with clear differences. One combination discussed pairs more development in one place with lower buildings and narrow alleys in another. Each option needs a simple drawing, comparable measures and conditions for delivery.
  3. Set streets and ground-level open space before estimating building capacity. The group proposed setting total road widths and reserving room for squares and green space, then estimating building footprints, storeys and total floor area. Track rooftop solar, greenery and water storage separately. Use block-by-block constraints to compare a few combinations for carbon reduction and climate adaptation.
  4. Prepare for the 16 December final presentation. The next set of materials includes one shared base map, option sketches for the blocks and a comparison sheet. The block options, thresholds, phasing and responsibilities still need confirmation. The studio deliverables are a presentation and a booklet.

From analysis to design

The instructors asked the team to build on its interim presentation. Use the study layers to explain existing conditions, then organize the design by what will change and who will manage it. Start from the terrain and existing plots. Set out the changes and operating needs for roads, paved surfaces and drainage, as well as trees, planted and water storage areas, buildings and transport.

  • Building renewal. Identify what to keep, retrofit or rebuild.
  • Streets and public space. Show how walking, green space, squares and ground-level activity fit together.
  • Transport system. Show how walking, cycling and motor traffic will work across the wider network once streets change.

For each part, explain what it does to cut emissions and adapt to climate risks. Tie the studies to the same sites. Building energy depends on roof use and the form of renewal. Stormwater links roofs, green spaces and the drainage network. Street design joins the cross-sections to vehicle and pedestrian flows. The group suggested marking the sites together on the existing 3D model and an A3 base map, checking conflicts, then refining the design within each study.

Identify who can take part and who can put changes into effect. The discussion included ward authorities and their departments, land and building owners, developers, residents, workers, shopkeepers, shoppers and diners. Tie these roles to individual blocks, street sections or projects.

Block options

The group first used the triangular block by Kanda Station to discuss three ways to renew buildings. It then considered the five blocks within the Primary Boundary and discussed keeping two or three distinct options where the site conditions supported them. Streets and access

  • Define the journeys to improve. The group discussed small shopping streets near Kanda Station entrances, local streets crossing the Primary Boundary, railway underpasses and spaces under bridges. Draw ground-floor uses, shade, places to pause and underpass lighting together. Follow walking routes beyond the study boundary to show where people come from and where they go, and how new street uses could change demand.
  • Start with the space available on each street. Where sidewalks already exist, check current use, future pedestrian demand and prospects for street-front shops before deciding whether to widen them. Narrower car lanes could free space within the existing right of way. The discussion mentioned 2.5 m lanes on some local roads, around 3.2 m on the streets being considered and a possible 1.0–1.2 m strip for planting or drainage. Use measured cross-sections to confirm widths, one-sided or two-sided layouts and where functions can share space.
  • Draw a shared cross-section. For two priority streets, the transport study considered reducing some lane space for walking and cycling. Check parking on one side, bus needs, planting and drainage. Share the available widths with the green space and drainage studies so all functions fit one drawing. A third, more complex road involves elevated infrastructure, a larger network of lanes and traffic signal timing. The available data and time determine how much to simulate.
  • Connect cycling routes. The group discussed links toward Shōwa-dōri and the Kanda River, including paths shared by people walking and cycling. One proposal was to use ground space under elevated infrastructure for planting, walking and cycling. Check how ground-level and elevated traffic would connect, who manages the roads and what changes would cost.
  • Test changes before permanent works. The group discussed a small, temporary street trial. Reduce vehicle space for a period, increase room for walking or staying and observe movement, footfall and use by shops before deciding on permanent works. Heisei-dōri and Kanda Kanamono-dōri are useful site references. Confirm the two test sections on the shared map.

Green space and roofs

  • Choose priority sites from the study maps. Start with blocks with higher temperatures or little shade and open space. Compare building age, height and renewal plans to find places for green roofs, planting or squares. Use land surface temperature (LST) to locate hot spots. Explain what ground-level squares, street trees and green roofs each contribute.
  • Update roof priorities with the building schemes. Check usable roof area, shade, soil depth, vegetation and the area treated when fitting greenery, solar photovoltaic panels (PV) and water storage. The group discussed using lower roofs for planting and sunnier higher roofs for solar power. Choose roof uses building by building and consider where uses can share a roof.
  • Plan temporary and long-term open spaces. Show temporary open space and how it would be managed while a plot awaits redevelopment. Where renewal is gradual, show how small open areas, historic alleys, street planting and uses can be retained or improved. Define daily uses, access routes, operators and opening hours for squares.
  • Connect stormwater measures to street sections. Street planting and drainage, rooftop storage and ground-level open space affect where water goes. Show the route of runoff, who maintains each space, and which dimensions and connections to the drainage network need checking. Assess heat and stormwater with separate measures.

Evaluation

State what each option should achieve and compare options on the same basis. The team still needs to confirm key performance indicator (KPI) values, how to weigh trade-offs and how to show results. The group discussed expressing different outcomes in monetary terms. The method remains open. The existing scenario workbench displays measures side by side in their own units.

Order for calculating space

  1. Mark streets and open space. On the map of all five blocks, confirm the space used by roads and the total street widths. Mark squares, ground-level greenery and other areas to keep clear of buildings.
  2. Estimate building floor area. Draw footprints and storeys for each option in the remaining area. Multiply the footprint by the number of storeys when floor plates are similar. Add area by storey when their sizes vary substantially.
  3. Set block constraints and study inputs. Enter allowed area, height and uses to retain for each block. Add the inputs for heat, stormwater, green space, energy and transport. Record tree locations and counts and roof uses as needed.
  4. Compare a few combinations first. Draw one sketch and set out the measures for each combination. Record which blocks save energy and when, along with benefits and conflicts that need further analysis. The group also discussed optimizing combinations across all blocks. The first set of representative comparisons can support the final presentation.

Governance and phases

  • Identify who can act on each plot. Distinguish landowners, holders of building rights, business operators and developers. Identify who can decide to rebuild a property or lease it. The meeting mentioned Nippon Steel Kowa Real Estate and Sumitomo Realty & Development. Check existing project records for the plots and their current status. Plot-level rights and new development intentions still need confirmation.
  • Plan for long-term care of public space. The group discussed when neighbourhood associations, business groups, landowners and public bodies might join, who could provide or rent space and who would organize events and daily maintenance. Confirm the responsible agencies and their powers for roads that cross jurisdictions and for elevated infrastructure.
  • Consider the effect of shifting floor area between plots. Height limits on some plots and more floor area elsewhere would change development opportunities for owners. The discussion raised air rights, allocation of floor area and compensation as possible ways to coordinate. Check which measures exist, when they can be used and what different owners and tenants would bear.
  • Support shops through renewal. Rising land prices or rents could affect whether existing shops can stay, especially where owners and shop operators are different. Set out temporary premises, ways for shops to return and management of public space. The group linked these arrangements to keeping the area active.
